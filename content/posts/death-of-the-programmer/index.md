title: "The death of the programmer"
date: 2026-07-27

I "hand wrote" code the other day. That is, I used my very own fingers to tap out Python code on a keyboard without any AI assistance. Normally, I would have used Claude Code or OpenCode for this task, but due to an administrative snafu at work my team temporarily lost access. The code was nothing exciting, but it fixed a real bug. It felt so good to write code again that I told my teammates about it, who promptly informed me there'd been a work-around for a few hours. No one actually said, "quit messing around, and get back work" but it was definitely implied.

I [used to think][1] that I could continue writing code and delegate the boring bits to the AI. This anecdote, if nothing else, highlights how badly that strategy has gone. Until recently, a software developer writing code was not only a good use of time, it _was_ the job. Now it's a waste of time. On a good day, I write better code than an AI, but the AI is usually good enough and it can certainly write it a lot faster than I can. Despite the costs associated with AI, it's still cheaper than a human writing code. You'd have to be crazy to hire anyone to write code right now, and I don't see that changing.

This is only the latest instance of an old process. When capitalism works well, efficient methods drive out less efficient ones. Consider your shoes for a moment. Before shoes were made in factories, each pair was hand-made by a craftsman. At least at first, I suspect the factory-made shoes weren't as good as what a local shoemaker produced, but this wasn't enough to keep the less efficient trade alive. Today, there's still a market for hand-made shoes, but it's far smaller than the factory-made variety, and likely persists on the benevolence of people who brag about their hand-made shoes. A market for artisanal software is unlikely to do so well, and I doubt we'll even keep a remnant of programmers as large as the shoemakers.

Personally, this is a tough blow. I love programming. I loved it when I was a kid writing BASIC, through all the languages and technologies I've used since. I'm certainly no AI-coding cheerleader, but even I need to accept that programming will no longer be part of my professional life. I could try to hang on to writing code a bit longer. After all, just because efficient methods tend to win out _eventually_, doesn't mean that the process is even. Certain niches will survive for quite a while, and some industries and sectors are so risk averse that it might be possible to make a career while they catch up. If I were closer to retirement, I'd probably pursue one of those options, but with at least another 20-years of work in front of me, this seems undesirable.

So, what's a middle-aged computer programmer do now? The obvious answer is to continue building software. After all, we haven't been "programmers" in a long time, we're "software developers" or "software engineers". We built software, which just happened to require programming. The day-to-day work is changing dramatically, but the output is still the same shape.

You may be wondering if software development sans programming is even a viable career path. After all, the robots did come for our jobs and they've already taken a significant part of it. But I think software development is as safe as anything right now. I'm not sure if it's human nature or a specific feature of society, but we want _more_. We don't want the same amount we had last year, and we certainly don't want less. So imagine you're a CEO of some corporation who's just been told that your AI software development pipeline is up and running. You can push for layoffs to increase the bottom line. Or you can push for new projects to increase the top line. I'm sure some will choose the bottom line, particularly if software development is a cost-center. But I expect enough will choose new revenue-generating projects that the total amount of software written will increase. Add to that new ventures, some of which will only be viable because it's cheaper to build software, and you have a lot more software than we did before.

The next question is who will be generating the software? First, let's consider that the users themselves will generate their software. After all, why buy software when you can tell an AI agent to generate it for you? This is already happening, but it's limited and I think it will remain limited. People buy all sorts of things they could technically make themselves, and software should be no different. For instance, even though I'm capable of making my own supper, I still eat at restaurants. Sometimes I want a better meal than I can make, or I need something quick when I'm out, or I simply don't feel like cooking. Software companies have every incentive to out-compete home-grown AI-generated software, and it probably won't be a close competition.

Another option, is that executives will tell the AI what to build, like a one-person software engineering department. But specifying software and validating that it meets the spec is still work, and almost certainly work that most executives would rather delegate. They currently delegate it to product managers, many of whom could easily take on a slightly broader role and generate the software. But I also think a lot of former software developers, testers and designers can do that job, and with lots of new products, we'll need more people in that role. My best guess is that the barriers between the current software development specialities will continue to erode until product, designers, devs and testers are all more-or-less homogeneous product people. If AI handles most of the details, what's the point of the specialization? Specialization seems to emerge on it's own from such an environment, but I doubt it will look like what we have today.

Of course, with the programming gone, all that's left are the parts of the job I never much liked. Feeding specs into an AI and checking the outputted software is definitely not the job I signed up for. It's not necessarily awful, and it might even provide it's own set of interesting challenges. I have some concerns about this new direction, but for now, at least, I'm making the most of it and working with my replacement.

If it turns out that I can't stand what software has become, I don't think the experience is entirely worthless on the market. Half-a-career spent working deeply on a computer, plus some specific training, should translate well enough to related disciplines (IT, cybersecurity, product training come to mind). There ought to be something we can do, even if it's unrelated to shipping software.

I'm disappointed with the way this has turned out, but I couldn't think of a better way to spend the first 20 years of my career than programming computers. I was lucky to have that. And I have to believe that there will continue to be a place for former programmers.
